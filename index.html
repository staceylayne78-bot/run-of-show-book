Run-of-Show Book

A design thinking toolkit tells you which tool to pick. It doesn't tell you how to run the room — what to say, how long each thing takes, when to vote, who decides. These are ten complete run-of-shows written out minute by minute, plus the method for assembling your own. Every step names the tool it uses, so you can print whichever version of that canvas your own toolkit already has.

Written for a team that already had a tool library and still couldn't reliably get through an hour. Most enterprise design groups have one: forty-odd canvases, each with prompts and a stated output, and nothing anywhere about the minute-to-minute. This is the missing layer, assembled from five outside systems and credited step by step. Take any of it.

What a run-of-show adds to a tool library

A toolkit is two things: a library of tools, and a method for outlining which of them to use. The good ones already carry the principle this book is built on — the output of a tool in one phase should generate the input of a tool in the next. What they stop short of is the hour itself.

A toolkit gives you

Dozens of tools across the phases, each with a canvas, prompts and a stated output. A method for outlining a process: identify the problem, define the opportunity, specify goals, consider constraints, choose phases, select tools, detail deliverables. And, if it's honest, permission to be non-linear.

This book adds

Timings per step. The words to say when you open an activity and when you cut a discussion off. Group sizes and who should be in the room. How the voting actually works. A named Decider. A commit step with owners and dates. And the handoff written out between every pair of steps.

So use them together

Pick the run-of-show that matches what the room needs to leave with. Print the canvases it names from whichever toolkit you have. Run the clock. When none of the ten fits, use Build your own — a toolkit's own outline method with the facilitation layer added.

Four habits underneath every run-of-show here. None of these are in any tool library I’ve worked from, and all four are why a session lands.
  • Work together, alone. Generate in silence, individually, before anything is discussed. The loudest person stops setting the agenda.
  • Vote instead of debating. Dots are faster than discussion and anonymous, so people mark what they think rather than what their manager thinks. Most toolkits have a version of this; the one I work from calls it Visualize the Vote.
  • Name a Decider. One person breaks ties and signs off. Votes inform them; they decide. Without this, workshops end in a follow-up meeting.
  • End with owners. Every output leaves with a name and a date on it, or it leaves marked "no owner yet" — said out loud, written on the wall.

What tool libraries usually don’t have

Forty-odd tools is a lot, and there are still whole kinds of session a design thinking toolkit has no tool for. These aren't flaws — a toolkit scopes itself deliberately — but they are the reason one process starts to feel like the only process. Each gap is a door into a different way of using what's already on the shelf. The module column points into the training plan below, which is how I closed each one.

Training plan

Seven modules, each one aimed at a gap above. The point isn't to adopt another company's system — it's to come back and use your own differently. Every module ends with the same question: what does this change about how I'd use the toolkit? Roughly 15 hours, self-paced; there's no prize for finishing it in three weeks.

Reps

Learning isn't running

Nothing on this page substitutes for having run a session once. That's why the progress marks below say "ran it," not "read it" — and why the modules are deliberately interleaved with the reps rather than stacked in front of them.

Cheap reps exist

Workshopr has twelve free 60-minute scripted sessions, no sign-in. Run one between modules when you want a rep without designing anything. The script is written; your only job is the room.

Teammates go up the ladder

Don't start someone new on a half-day session. Shadow, then one tool, then a short session, then co-facilitate. The teaching ladder has the detail.

Five reps, in this order

Lowest risk first — which is deliberately not the order the run-of-shows chain in. Learn them backwards, run them forwards. Each rep is a real session with a real outcome; practicing on an invented problem teaches the steps but not the room.

Readiness

What has to be true before the session is worth holding. Three of these are gates — if one fails, the right move is to not run it. The rest are fixable, but only before the invite goes out. The toolkit has nothing on this: it begins at the first tool, which assumes someone already decided the session should happen.

The three gates

Gate 1

The question is actually open

A workshop where the answer is already chosen is a ratification with sticky notes, and rooms can smell it inside twenty minutes. Once they do, you don't get the room back — and you don't get the next one either.

How to test itAsk the sponsor: “if the room lands somewhere you don't expect, what changes?” If the answer is nothing, you've been asked to stage a decision, not make one.
Gate 2

The Decider is in the room, knows they are, and stays

Not a representative. Not someone who'll “take it back.” A session that ends in a recommendation to a person who wasn't there produces a document, and documents don't get decided — they get circulated.

How to test itCan you name them? Have you told them, in those words? Are they there for the whole thing, not just the last half hour?
Gate 3

The output has somewhere to go on Monday

Every workshop produces something. Whether it survives the week depends on whether receiving work already exists — a backlog, a brief, a deck with a date on it. If there's nowhere for it to land, you're producing a wall photo.

How to test itName the person who takes the output and the date they need it. If you can't, move the session later, not earlier.
The one that fails most often

The room doesn't know what's already been tried

Ideation circles the drain of what we already know. It reads as a facilitation problem — the exercise didn't land, the group was low-energy, somebody dominated. It usually isn't. A mixed-depth room doesn't average out to medium depth. It floors to the shallowest shared knowledge, because that's the only ground everyone can stand on. Three mechanisms do the flooring, and they're worth knowing separately because they need different fixes.

People can only recombine what they hold. Ideas are a function of inputs. Nobody in the room is holding back the good idea; most of them don't have the material to build it from. Asking harder, or asking in silence, doesn't add material.
Voting rewards legibility, not novelty. In a heat map, an idea everyone understands collects dots and an idea one person understands doesn't. Novel ideas lose structurally, before anyone has judged them on merit — and the room reads the result as consensus.
The person who knows stays quiet. Saying “we tried that in 2023, here's what killed it” takes four minutes. Silent writing gives ninety seconds and a sticky note. So the deepest knowledge in the room is exactly the knowledge the format can't accept, and the expert learns to sit on it.

What to do about it, in order of leverage

Name the dead ideas out loud, first. Ten minutes at the open: here are the five things that come up every time, and what stopped each one. Write them on a sheet headed already explored and leave it up all session. You're not banning them — you're making it cost something to re-propose one. This is the single move that stops the circling, and it costs you ten minutes.
Write the explored-territory brief. One or two pages, circulated before: what's been tried, what happened, why it stopped. Stops, not successes — the reasons things died are the useful part and the part nobody writes down. Your toolkit has no tool for this. It is the highest-value page you could add to it.
Move Precursors & Analogs out of the session. At it gets twenty minutes, which surfaces what's top of mind — which is the drain again, one step removed. Assign it a week out instead: three examples each, from outside your own industry, with a line on what the example actually does. Then run the in-session version as the share-out only.
Give the expert a role, not a turn. The cheap version: hand them a visible card and make them the “that's been tried” caller, with standing permission to interrupt. The expensive version is a live Expert Interview at the open — which transfers what they know into everyone's hands before the generating starts, but means scheduling experts weeks out, so it's a choice about planning time rather than a default. A role they can play beats a turn they'll decline.
Vote on problems, not solutions, when depth is uneven. Everyone in a mixed room can rank a problem by how much it hurts. Not everyone can judge a novel solution. This is the move Lightning Decision Jam is built on, and it's why that format works in rooms where ideation doesn't.
Split the vote into two passes.not from a source One heat map for this is new to us, one for we could actually do this. The overlap is the interesting set. The feasibility-only cluster is the drain made visible — which means you can point at it instead of arguing with it.

Expectations

Readiness is your decision: is this session worth holding. Expectations are everyone else's: what they think they're walking into and what they'll judge the result against. Nearly every workshop described afterwards as “great session, nothing happened” passed the readiness gate and failed here. Nobody was told what the session would not do, so it got measured against a thing it was never going to produce.

Six expectations to lower on purpose

Rooms arrive carrying these whether or not anyone said them. Each one is cheap to defuse in the opening frame and expensive to discover at the close.

What they assumeSay instead
Everything raised today will get decided“We're deciding one thing today. Everything else goes on that sheet and I'll tell you where it went.”
Consensus is the goal“We're not looking for agreement, we're looking for a decision. Pat decides. Your job is to make sure Pat decides with everything in front of them.”
My idea will get built“Most of what we generate today won't get built, including most of mine. What we're protecting is that the thing that does get built was chosen against the right problem.”
We'll leave with a design“Nothing we draw today is a design. It's a way of arguing about the same thing at the same time.”
Attending means being consulted on everything downstream“Today's input is on the problem and the direction. The build decisions will not come back to this group, and I'd rather say that now than let you find out in March.”
Silence means agreement“If you haven't said anything in twenty minutes I'm going to ask you directly, and that's not a trap.”
The one sentence that does the most work. “Here is what we will not do today.” It's absent from almost every workshop opening, it takes fifteen seconds, and it converts the most common post-workshop complaint into something that was agreed in advance by everyone in the room.
The sponsor contract is the one to not skip. The opening frame protects the session. The sponsor contract protects you. When a stakeholder says six weeks later that the workshop was supposed to produce a roadmap, the page you both agreed is the difference between a disagreement and a problem.

Rank in the room

The Decider model assumes a room of near-peers who happen to need a tiebreaker. In a large organization it almost never is. The Decider is usually two or three levels above most of the room, and every mechanic in the method quietly stops working — not loudly enough to notice in the session, only afterwards when the output looks like what the senior person already thought. This is the largest gap between AJ&Smart's rooms and yours, and none of the three source systems addresses it.

What rank does to the mechanics

It contaminates the vote. A heat map in front of a senior Decider stops measuring what the room thinks and starts measuring what the room thinks the Decider thinks. The dots still cluster, so it still looks like it worked.
It contaminates generation. A concept on the wall with a senior name on it wins before anyone reads it. Silent writing protects against anchoring, not against authorship.
It silences problem-naming. The problems most worth hearing are usually the ones that implicate somebody senior who is sitting in the room. Nobody writes that note.
It turns participation into performance. People stop trying to solve the thing and start trying to be seen solving the thing. These look identical for about an hour.

Six fixes, cheapest first

The Decider doesn't heat-map, and votes last. AJ&Smart already say this. It's the first rule dropped when the room is senior, which is precisely when it carries the weight.
Concepts go up unsigned. You collect and transcribe them, or everyone uses the same marker and nobody writes a name. Authorship is the biggest single source of rank contamination and the cheapest one to remove.
The Decider speaks last in every round. Agreed with them beforehand, said out loud at the open so the room can hold them to it. If the senior person speaks first, the round is over and the next forty minutes are theatre.
The Decider doesn't do the exercises. They watch, they ask questions, they decide. A senior person's sticky notes aren't data, they're instructions wearing a sticky note.
Collect the problems before the session, anonymously. If the real problem is that a program has no owner, nobody will write it with that owner in the room. Gather them by form a week out, de-duplicate, and put them up in your handwriting.
Two-tier Decider when the gap is more than two levels. Delegate session authority to someone closer to the work, and brief the senior person before and after. Get the delegation in one written line in the sponsor contract — “Dana decides in the room and I'll back it” — because without it the delegation collapses the first time somebody appeals upward, and it will be appealed.
Name it out loud at the open. “Pat is more senior than most of us in this room. That's why Pat votes last and doesn't take part in the heat map.” Ten seconds, and it tells the room the asymmetry was designed for rather than ignored — which is the difference between a rule that looks like deference and one that looks like method.
The opposite failure. A Decider too junior to make the decision stick is worse than a senior one, because the session produces a decision that gets quietly reversed and everyone learns the workshop didn't count. The test is one question: can this person say yes without going to ask someone? If not, they aren't the Decider — they're a participant, and you're missing gate 2.

What goes on the wall

Most of these sessions hang off a spine — one visual the whole room works against. The spine is a design decision, not a default, and it determines the kind of feedback you get far more than the exercises do. A journey across touchpoints and a screen-by-screen flow produce different sessions from the same room on the same problem, and picking the wrong one is the quietest way to waste a morning.

Altitude

The zoom level you draw the journey at sets the zoom level of the ideas that come out of it. This is the most reliable rule in the book and the one least often used on purpose: ideas arrive one rung below the altitude you drew. Draw at task altitude and you get interaction fixes, every time, no matter how the ideation exercise is run. Rooms then conclude they aren't creative, when what happened is that they were handed a wall that could only produce small answers.

Two rules that follow. First: draw the journey one rung above the decision you need, because the ideas land a rung lower. If you need service-design decisions, map the episode, not the task. Second: never mix altitudes on one wall. A journey with “decides to trust us with their money” three cards along from “clicks continue” produces a room that argues for an hour while believing it agrees — and the sticky notes will sort themselves to the lowest rung on the wall, because that's the one everybody can be concrete about.
The cost of screens. Put real-looking screens on the wall and the room will react to the visual design — type, color, button placement — and not the flow, no matter what you asked for. Two fixes, use both: show them deliberately rough, and put up a separate sheet headed visual notes so those comments have somewhere to go. People aren't being difficult; they're commenting on the thing they can see, and if you give them nowhere to put it they'll keep saying it.

When a session doesn't flow

Your toolkit states the principle on its own process page: the output of a tool in one phase should generate the input of a tool in the next. It states it once and then never gives you a way to check it. So the most common way a well-planned session fails is that each activity ran fine on its own and the materials coming off one wall weren't what the next activity needed to pick up. Nobody can point at the moment it went wrong, because it didn't go wrong at a moment — it went wrong in a gap.

That's why every step in all ten run-of-shows ends with a line saying what it hands the next one. Those aren't commentary. They're the thing to check when you compose a new session, and the thing to audit when an old one didn't work.

The audit

Take the agenda. For every adjacent pair of activities, answer two questions in writing. If you can't name the artifact in a noun phrase — “a ranked list of six problems,” “one agreed sentence,” “three posters with a metric each” — the handoff was broken before the session started.

Question 1

What physically comes off the wall at the end of A?

Not what the room learned or felt. What exists: how many of what, in what form, at what altitude, chosen or unchosen.

Question 2

Is that literally the thing B's first instruction tells people to pick up?

Read B's opening sentence aloud. If it says “take your top three problems” and A produced forty unranked notes, B's first five minutes are a vote nobody budgeted for, and the session is now behind and improvising.

The run-of-shows

Each is a complete session. The first four chain in the order you already work: Blueprint the Service surfaces the problems, Frame the Challenge turns them into a Problem Statement and a How Might We, Ideas to Concepts answers it, Sequence the Work orders what survives. Strategy Signal sits above all four and sets the direction they serve — run it when nobody can say where you're going in two years. One Owner is the odd one out, built for a room of one or two people who have never done this. Seven through ten are the ones you reach for when the chain is not what you need: a stuck team or a retro, an ideation that ended without enough information, a concept that has to become requirements, and the forty-five minutes that finds out how it fails.

Which means you can skip the front of number 2 when you’ve come straight from number 1 — the research readout and Affinity Mapping steps are there for a cold start, and you won’t have one. Start at the Problem Statement.

The transferable lesson. Facilitating a room of one strips out everything that exists to manage a group and leaves only the part that's about helping someone see their own situation from outside it. Build your own

Steps 1 through 7 are the outlining method most design thinking toolkits already carry — problem, opportunity, goals, constraints, phases, tools, deliverables. Steps 8 through 10 are the facilitation layer they leave out, and they are the difference between an agenda and a run-of-show. Do the first seven before you touch a calendar invite.

  1. Identify the problem.Write a Problem Statement. It guides every phase after this, so the time spent here comes back. If you can't write it yet, that's what run-of-show 1 is for.
  2. Define the opportunity.Respond to the Problem Statement with a How Might We. Get stakeholder alignment on both before you go further — every toolkit is emphatic about this, and it's still the step people skip.
  3. Specify your goals.What do you want out of the process? Attach a number where you can, so you can tell afterwards whether you got it. Importance vs. Difficulty or the Innovation Ambition Matrix will rank them if stakeholders disagree.
  4. Consider your constraints.Time, money, what's already been researched, brand standards, anything that makes a plan unexecutable. Revisit these as you go and update them when they change.
  5. Choose your phases.You do not have to use every phase. Goals and constraints will tell you to shorten one or skip it. Starting at analyze because research already exists is a legitimate opening, not a shortcut.
  6. Select your tools.One tool per slot. The rule that makes a session work: the output of a tool in one phase should generate the input of a tool in the next. If it doesn't, you have two activities rather than a sequence.
  7. Detail your deliverables.Write down what each tool will produce. If a tool's deliverable doesn't move you closer to a solution, stop and rethink the approach rather than running it anyway.
  8. Write the handoff between each pair out loud.Finish the sentence "step A hands step B ___". This is step 6 made concrete, and it's the fastest way to find the missing step in a draft agenda.
  9. Add the fixed furniture.Ten minutes to open and frame. A break every ninety minutes. Fifteen to twenty minutes to commit with owners and dates. Not optional, and not where you save time.
  10. Total the times, add 20%, then cut a tool.Every first draft is too full. The version you run should look slightly too short on paper. It won't be. And write the Decider's name on the agenda before the session, not during it.

Teach it

How to hand a tool or a whole run-of-show to someone else. Most of this is instruction-giving, because that's where new facilitators lose a room — not in the design.

What · Why · How

Explain every tool in that order, every time. What we're about to do, why it helps us, how it works step by step. People who hear "how" before "why" spend the activity wondering what the point is.

Say it three times

Anything important, three times, three different ways. You've heard your own instructions dozens of times. The room is hearing them once, while thinking about something else.

One way only

Show a single way to run the tool. Options feel generous and land as confusion. Offer an alternative only if somebody asks.

Show, don't tell

Point at a filled-in canvas before anyone starts. The toolkit prints an example on most tool pages — use it rather than describing the output in the abstract.

Close with the insight

After the detail, one sentence of why: "The big idea is that we vote before we discuss, so the quiet ideas survive." That's what people remember and repeat.

Park, don't block

When someone raises something real but off-topic, write it on the parking lot out loud, then actually return to it. The visible note is what lets them let go of it.

Teaching ladder

Don't start a teammate on a whole session. Four steps, each a real session with real stakes, getting bigger.

StepThey doYou do
1 · ShadowSit in a session you facilitate with the run-of-show in hand, noting every moment they'd have been unsure.Debrief their list afterwards. That list is their curriculum.
2 · Run one toolFacilitate a single tool inside your session — Visualize the Vote or 6-to-1 Ideation first, because the instructions are short and the output is obviously right.Hand the room over explicitly and take it back explicitly. Don't rescue them mid-tool.
3 · Run a short sessionFacilitate a whole 60–90 minute session with a friendly group on a low-stakes topic.Be in the room as a participant, not a co-facilitator. Give feedback on one thing, not five.
4 · Co-facilitate a real oneSplit a half-day run-of-show with you, each taking whole phases.Take the phases they're least ready for, and tell them which those are and why.
